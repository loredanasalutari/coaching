## Current professional focus

There are moments in a career when things stop making sense.

You may still be competent, experienced, and reliable, yet the environment around you has changed. Decisions feel misaligned. Your role no longer matches your values. What once worked no longer does.

My coaching work developed around professionals in this kind of transition: people who want to regain clarity, agency, and direction without becoming someone they are not.

This work is not about motivation or positive thinking. It is about restoring coherence between who a person is, what they do, and the context in which they operate.

Today, I retain this coaching perspective alongside my work in IT. I am open to selected professional opportunities and conversations rather than operating a fixed coaching programme or public booking practice.

## When work no longer feels stable

People often begin looking for clarity when they recognize situations such as:

* their role changed, but nobody explained what that means for them;
* they remain competent, yet feel increasingly invisible;
* they sense tension, politics, or exclusion that is never addressed openly;
* staying once felt safer, but now feels like being stuck;
* they are still functioning, but disconnected from their work;
* responsibilities and authority no longer correspond;
* their work environment no longer reflects who they are;
* they do not want an unstable environment to redefine their identity.

These situations are not always dramatic from the outside. Someone may still be performing well while feeling increasingly disconnected from their role. Sometimes the question is whether to stay, leave, redefine the role, or find a different way of working.

The people who have found my perspective most relevant include:

* professionals navigating restructuring, layoffs, or organizational change;
* people experiencing a loss of meaning or misalignment at work;
* experienced professionals who feel undervalued, sidelined, or invisible;
* individuals dealing with power dynamics, burnout, or ethical tension;
* people questioning whether to stay, leave, or redefine their role;
* HR leaders and teams supporting employees during change.

Some arrive exhausted. Others arrive clear but blocked. What they often share is a desire to regain agency and act with integrity rather than through endless compliance or reflexive rebellion.

## What I do — and what I do not do

I do not motivate people to tolerate what is unsustainable. I do not push quick decisions, offer predefined paths, or tell someone what they *should* want. I do not try to optimize individuals so that they fit dysfunctional systems, and I do not position myself as the expert on another person's life.

I help slow complex situations down enough to understand them.

The work may explore:

* what is happening in the professional environment;
* how that environment affects behaviour, confidence, and choices;
* the tension between expectations and reality;
* the difference between responsibilities and actual authority;
* the relationship between personal values and professional decisions;
* what belongs to the surrounding system and what belongs to the individual;
* what is within a person's control and what is not;
* which choices are genuinely available now.

From there, deliberate decisions become possible again.

## How I work

My approach is conversational, structured, reflective, and adaptive. The aim is to create enough space around a difficult situation to see what is actually happening.

Most professional difficulties are not caused by a lack of skill. They often arise when different layers of professional life drift apart:

* expectations and reality;
* responsibilities and authority;
* values and decisions;
* identity and role.

When those layers become misaligned, people may experience confusion, loss of energy, or self-doubt even when nothing appears to be wrong on paper.

My work has drawn on:

* structured questioning;
* analytical and visual frameworks;
* purpose-oriented coaching;
* Neuro-Linguistic Programming models;
* reflective and guided conversational techniques;
* hypnotic language patterns when appropriate.

These are tools rather than formulas. They are selected according to how a person thinks, processes information, and responds. The work stays grounded in clear objectives, concrete situations, and real constraints rather than abstract ideals.

Questions that often guide reflection include:

* What has changed?
* What no longer fits?
* Who is doing what, in which context, and with what effect?
* What belongs to the system, and what belongs to you?
* What is within your control?
* What options are actually available?
* What would a coherent next step look like?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is coaching in my approach?

Coaching is a structured, reflective partnership focused on clarity, alignment, and conscious decision-making. It can help a person understand where work, role, values, or identity have become misaligned and consider how to move forward without betraying themselves.

It is not advice-giving, motivational talk, or performance pressure.

### Is this therapy or psychological treatment?

No. Coaching does not diagnose or treat mental-health conditions. It focuses on the present and future: choices, direction, and responsibility.

Coaching may coexist with work undertaken with a qualified mental-health professional, but it does not replace psychological or medical care.

### Who is this perspective most relevant to?

It is especially relevant to professionals in transition, people experiencing loss of meaning or misalignment at work, individuals navigating power dynamics or ethical tension, and teams supporting people during organizational change.

### How are conversations structured?

Conversations are focused and reflective. Depending on the person and the topic, they may include structured questioning, visual mapping, NLP-based models, or reflective language patterns. Each conversation has a clear intention, and useful reflection often continues afterwards.

### Do I need to know exactly what I want?

No. Many people begin with a feeling rather than a goal:

* “Something is off.”
* “I cannot stay like this.”
* “I do not know what the next step is.”

Clarity is often an outcome of the process, not a prerequisite.

### Which languages can be used?

English, Italian, or Brazilian Portuguese.

### Do I work with organizations as well as individuals?

My experience can be relevant to individual conversations, workshops, group sessions, or transition-support initiatives. Any new collaboration is considered individually rather than offered through a standard programme.

### Fear is not the problem. Indecision is.

*Originally published 12 October 2024.*

Many capable people know what they could do next: change role, speak up, invest in growth, or leave a situation that no longer fits. And yet they hesitate.

They call it fear:

* fear of wasting money;
* fear of choosing incorrectly;
* fear of discovering uncomfortable truths;
* fear of changing something that still “sort of works”.

Fear becomes the explanation, but fear is not necessarily the real issue.

#### Fear is a signal, not an obstacle

Fear is a natural response when we approach the boundary of what we know. It appears when we are about to leave a familiar role, when an old identity no longer fits, or when the next step requires us to redefine ourselves.

If fear disappeared entirely, growth could become reckless. The aim is therefore not to eliminate fear, but to decide while fear is present.

#### Why confidence does not come first

Many people wait to feel confident before acting. This reverses the usual sequence. Confidence is not the opposite of fear; it is often what follows movement.

When we remain still, fear has time to grow, imagination fills the gaps, and indecision begins to feel safer than choice. When we move, even imperfectly, confidence can reorganize itself around action.

#### The paralysis of staying inside the known

What keeps people stuck is not always fear of the unknown. It can be attachment to familiar discomfort, known dissatisfaction, and roles they already know how to survive in.

Staying feels controllable. Leaving feels undefined. But undefined does not necessarily mean dangerous; it may simply mean not yet mapped.

Instead of asking only, “Why am I afraid?”, another useful question is:

> What decision am I postponing?

Fear rarely disappears through analysis alone. It changes when direction becomes clearer.

Professional stagnation often comes not from a lack of skill but from staying too long in misaligned environments, postponing important conversations, or waiting for external permission.

You may not need more courage. You may need a clearer decision. Fear can adjust afterwards.

### When your job title is no longer who you are

*Originally published 26 October 2024.*

Many professionals feel unexpected discomfort when updating a profile on a platform such as LinkedIn. It is not because they lack skills or experience, but because the available words no longer fit.

They hesitate, rewrite the headline several times, and postpone clicking “save”. What is happening underneath may not be a writing problem at all. It may be an identity shift.

#### Role is not identity

A job title describes a role. A professional identity describes a contribution.

Profiles often use a formula such as **Name — Role at Company**. It is familiar, but it anchors a person's value to a temporary position. When roles change, organizations merge, or careers evolve, the profile can begin to feel fragile or false.

A more resilient approach is to describe what someone contributes, not only where they currently sit.

Compare:

> Software Requirements Engineer at Company X

with:

> Software professional specializing in complex, safety-critical systems, working at the intersection of stakeholders, constraints, and long-term reliability.

The second description does not deny the current role. It places the role in a larger professional context and communicates expertise, thinking style, and value without tying identity to a single organizational structure.

#### Why this matters during change

During restructuring, mergers, layoffs, or internal shifts, professionals may realize that their online presence reflects where they were rather than who they are becoming.

This can create questions such as:

* If I change my profile, what does that imply about loyalty?
* If I do not change it, am I hiding?
* Who am I professionally beyond this role?

These are not only branding questions. They are questions of clarity and identity.

#### Discretion and digital exposure

Profiles overloaded with specific internal details, tools, processes, or sensitive responsibilities can also increase exposure to targeted phishing or social engineering.

Clarity does not require overexposure. Precision does not require disclosure. Describing contribution rather than internal specifics can support both professional positioning and digital discretion.

An “About” section works best as a mirror rather than a performance. It can answer quieter questions:

* How do you approach complexity?
* In which environments do you do your best work?
* How do you collaborate, decide, or navigate uncertainty?

Potential collaborators and employers are not only scanning for skills; they are also sensing fit.

If updating a professional profile feels uncomfortable, that discomfort may be information. It may signal that professional identity has evolved faster than the current role or the language used to describe it.

A useful closing question is:

> Who am I allowed to be professionally, beyond my current title?

### Why unclear thinking keeps you stuck in the wrong professional life

*Originally published 6 April 2025.*

> “Language reflects the way we think. If language is vague, thinking will be vague.” — Bandler & Grinder, *The Structure of Magic*

Many professionals describe their situation with a familiar sentence:

> Something is wrong, but I cannot quite say what.

They may not be unhappy enough to leave or aligned enough to stay. They feel tension, fatigue, or irritation but lack the words to frame it. This is not necessarily a motivation problem. It is often a clarity problem.

#### Vague language creates vague decisions

People frequently speak in abstract, impersonal, or unfinished sentences:

* “My work situation is difficult.”
* “There is a problem with my role.”
* “The environment does not feel right.”

These sentences sound meaningful, but they hide responsibility and agency. A role is not problematic by itself, and an environment does not act on its own. Usually, specific behaviours, expectations, decisions, or boundaries are misaligned. Without more precise language, the mind cannot locate where change might be possible.

As long as the problem remains unnamed, it remains difficult to touch.

#### The professional cost of unclear thinking

When thinking stays vague, people tend to postpone decisions, tolerate dissatisfaction longer than necessary, adapt instead of choose, and hope that a situation will resolve itself.

Over time, this may erode confidence:

* “Maybe I am the problem.”
* “Maybe I should be grateful.”
* “Maybe this is simply how it is.”

Clarity is not about being harsh. It is about being accurate.

#### Propositional thinking

Propositional thinking is the ability to structure thoughts in complete, logical statements: someone acts, something happens, and a consequence follows.

It allows us to ask:

* Who does what?
* In which context?
* With which impact?

This kind of thinking is developed through writing, reflection, and dialogue. When it is replaced by fragments, emotional shortcuts, borrowed narratives, or passive content consumption, professional identity can begin to feel blurred—not because it is weak, but because it is poorly articulated.

#### A bridge to the NLP Meta Model

In Neuro-Linguistic Programming, the Meta Model examines how language loses precision through:

* deletions, such as “something is wrong”;
* generalizations, such as “it is always like this”;
* distortions, such as “this role defines who I am”.

Restoring linguistic precision can restore choice. When people learn to name what they do, what they tolerate, and what they want to change, they may stop feeling completely trapped and begin to see options.

#### A practical invitation

You do not need to change your life today, but you can begin by naming it more precisely.

Try this:

1. Take a pen and paper.
2. Write one sentence about your current professional situation.
3. Rewrite it until it contains a clear subject, a concrete action or situation, and an observable effect on you.

Do this not to publish or judge the sentence, but simply to see it.

Every professional transition begins not only with courage or confidence, but with more accurate language. If you can name what is happening, you can begin to decide what to do with it.
